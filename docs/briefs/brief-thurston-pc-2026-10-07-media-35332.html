Planners weigh new land rules for fish and wildlife habitats

Official Title: NEW DRAFT - CAO Chapter 24.25: FWHCAs (all changes) | Docket: Thurston County • 2026-10-07 | Stage: Advisory Review

For Staff

  • Buffer Methodology Gap (Staff Report, p. 7): The draft applies 200-foot Site Potential Tree Height (SPTH) 200-year buffers based on WDFW Riparian Guidance (2020), but does not clarify whether the requirement applies to non-fishbearing ephemeral swales on private residential parcels under one acre.

Why This Matters Now

The Thurston County Planning Commission is currently holding an advisory review. This is the earliest and most direct window for public input to reshape draft code before formal recommendations are submitted to the Thurston County Board of County Commissioners. This proposal affects rural landowners, residential builders, and agricultural parcels across unincorporated Thurston County. The draft establishes expanded stream protection zones and introduces new standards regulating residential yard care and prairie soils. While state fish and wildlife guidelines recommend Best Available Science, the Board of County Commissioners retains policy discretion over local residential exceptions.

What Changes

Subject Current Code Proposed Change Authority (State Mandate vs. County Choice)
Riparian Buffers Standard fixed stream buffers (TCC 24.25.020) Site Potential Tree Height (SPTH) 200-year management zones (Staff Memo p. 4) State Guidance / County Discretion (WDFW recommends SPTH; adoption into local code is chosen by county)
Prairie Soil Protection General habitat review (TCC 24.25.040) Prescriptive lawn size limits and native seedbank preservation (Presentation p. 4) County Choice (Specific residential landscape limits are local policy choices)

What's Good Here

  • Habitat Connectivity: Encourages clustered development to preserve contiguous open space and wildlife corridors (Presentation p. 3).
  • Stream Temperature Protection: Protects cold-water salmon stream corridors through science-based riparian management zones (Staff Memo p. 2).

Our Reading

  • Ownership: Narrows private parcel management by expanding stream buffers under Site Potential Tree Height formulas and regulating residential lawn area in prairie soils (Presentation p. 4). Our reading: This approach rests on the premise that residential yards and small rural acreage must be managed primarily as regional habitat reserves, placing the burden of regional species recovery directly on existing homeowners.
  • Subsidiarity: Shifts local riparian standards toward state agency management formulas driven by Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife guidance (Staff Memo p. 2). Our reading: Relying on standardized state agency tree-height models assumes uniform forest conditions across diverse local watersheds, leaving county residents with fewer locally tailored variance paths.
  • Small and local vs. large and distant: Concentrates fixed biological evaluation and habitat management plan costs on individual lot owners compared to institutional developers (Presentation p. 5). Our reading: Conditioning routine residential permits on third-party ecological reports assumes applicants can absorb up-front consulting overhead, which disproportionately burdens families building or repairing a single home.

What It's Trying to Do, and How Supporters Would Answer

The Goal: The draft revisions aim to preserve cold-water salmon streams and rare native prairie habitats across Thurston County (Staff Memo p. 1). Protecting native fish runs and watershed health is a worthy and widely shared community goal.

How Supporters Would Answer Our Concerns: Supporters argue that science-based Site Potential Tree Height buffers and prairie soil rules are required to prevent irreversible habitat fragmentation as rural areas develop. If no action is taken, stream temperatures will continue to rise, threatening treaty-protected salmon runs and triggering state legal challenges under the Growth Management Act.

Goal vs. Means: Our concern is with the means, not the goal: conserving riparian corridors and prairies is right, but doing so through uncompensated restrictions on ordinary residential yards and costly consulting mandates places a public conservation duty on individual families rather than using voluntary stewardship incentives.

What Would Make It Better

  • Possible Ask (Separate state-mandated requirements from locally chosen additions): Request that staff clearly separate requirements strictly mandated by state statute or administrative rules from optional local policy additions, and evaluate whether the local extras are necessary.
  • Possible Ask (Protect existing legal uses, homes, yards, and gardens): Add an explicit safe-harbor provision confirming that existing legally established residential homes, maintained yards, gardens, and customary outbuildings are exempt from retroactive buffer expansions or new ongoing maintenance restrictions.
  • Possible Ask (Provide an accessible reasonable-use exception path for small or constrained lots): Include an administrative, low-cost reasonable-use exception process so that owners of small, historic, or legally platted lots who cannot meet full expanded buffers can still build a modest single-family home without costly quasi-judicial hearing fees.

What to Do

Who Decides Next: The Thurston County Planning Commission is an advisory citizen body that conducts public reviews and makes recommendations. It does not pass ordinances. The Thurston County Board of County Commissioners decides next whether to adopt the code into law.

Meeting & Next Step: The body reviewed this draft at its meeting on 2026-10-07. Next step not yet posted.

How to Comment: Submit written testimony online or by email to the staff liaison or clerk prior to the meeting cutoff (Check meeting agenda for registration cutoff), or sign up to testify at scheduled public hearings.
