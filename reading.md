# Further Reading

The Ledger asks a few plain questions of local decisions: Is it decided close to home? Does it help ordinary families own and keep a place? Who pays? These are the books and essays behind those questions, plus a few that disagree with us. None of it is required. Start with the first section.

## Start here

**[Localism: Coming Home to Catholic Social Teaching](https://www.chesterton.org/store/product/localism-coming-home-to-catholic-social-teaching/)**, Dale Ahlquist and Michael Warren Davis. A recent, readable case for local ownership, local work, and local government.

**[Small Is Beautiful: Economics as if People Mattered](https://centerforneweconomics.org/envision/legacy/ernst-friedrich-schumacher/small-is-beautiful/)**, E. F. Schumacher (1973). On scale, and why bigger is not always better for the people it is supposed to serve.

**[Chattanooga Civics.](https://chattanoogacivics.com/)** A local project in Chattanooga, Tennessee that helps residents see what their city and county governments are doing and how to respond. It is a good model for learning how your own government works, and it is close to what the Ledger tries to do.

## The tradition

**The Outline of Sanity**, G. K. Chesterton (1926). The classic case for widely held property and for small, local things.

**[What's Wrong with the World](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1717)**, G. K. Chesterton (1910). On the family, the home, and why ordinary people should own a stake in their place.

**[The Servile State](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64882)**, Hilaire Belloc (1912). How concentrated wealth and a large government can grow together, with ordinary people dependent on both.

**An Essay on the Restoration of Property**, Hilaire Belloc (1936). Belloc's proposals for spreading ownership more widely.

**Distributism Basics: Foundational Principles**, David W. Cooney (2020). A modern, plain-language introduction. His articles are free online at [practicaldistributism.com](https://practicaldistributism.com/distributism-basics/).

## Church teaching on property and subsidiarity

**[Rerum Novarum](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_l-xiii_enc_15051891_rerum-novarum.html)**, Pope Leo XIII (1891). The foundation of modern Catholic social teaching, on work, property, and the family.

**[Quadragesimo Anno](https://www.vatican.va/content/pius-xi/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_p-xi_enc_19310515_quadragesimo-anno.html)**, Pope Pius XI (1931). The classic statement of subsidiarity: higher bodies should help lower ones, not take over their work.

**[Centesimus Annus](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_01051991_centesimus-annus.html)**, Pope John Paul II (1991). A look at markets, property, and the state after the fall of communism.

**[Laudato Si'](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/encyclicals/documents/papa-francesco_20150524_enciclica-laudato-si.html)**, Pope Francis (2015). On caring for the natural world. It is a useful reminder that this tradition takes the land and water seriously, which matters for items like wetlands and stream buffers.

## Place, land, and local government

**[The Unsettling of America](https://www.counterpointpress.com/books/the-unsettling-of-america/)**, Wendell Berry (1977). On land, farming, and what is lost when a place is managed from far away.

**[Governing the Commons](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/governing-the-commons/A8BB63BC4A1433A50A3FB92EDBBB97D5)**, Elinor Ostrom (1990). How communities have managed shared resources like water and fisheries without waiting for a distant authority.

**[Strong Towns](https://www.strongtowns.org/book)**, Charles Marohn (2019). A practical look at how cities pay for themselves and how land-use rules affect ordinary households. It does not come from the distributist tradition, but it asks many of our questions about cost and who pays.

**[The Death and Life of Great American Cities](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/86058/the-death-and-life-of-great-american-cities-by-jane-jacobs/)**, Jane Jacobs (1961). On why small businesses, short blocks, and old buildings matter to a neighborhood.

## Where we might be wrong

**["What's Wrong with Distributism,"](https://mises.org/mises-daily/whats-wrong-distributism)** Thomas Woods (2002), on the Mises Institute website. A well-known critique of the whole approach.

**"Credit Union Economics,"** Michael McCabe. A distributist reply to Woods, available at [practicaldistributism.com](https://practicaldistributism.com/2023/02/16/credit-union-economics/). Read the two together.
